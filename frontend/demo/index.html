        Why profit and cash differ, month by month

        Two questions a CFO should ask before trusting any forecast.

        1 · What is it based on? (Assumptions) The forecast is a calculation, not a guess: every input is listed below with its value and where it comes from, and how it is updated each week.

        2 · Has it been right before? (Track record) We hid the real future of each scenario, made the forecast on 28 September, then compared. Like a weather forecast checked against the weather that actually came.

        1 · Assumptions: what the forecast is based on

        How assumptions are kept up to date

        Every weekThe engine re-reads bank balances, invoices and payments and recomputes how each customer pays. Recent payments weigh more: one from two months ago counts half as much as one from today.
        Unpaid overdue invoices countAn invoice 40 days overdue tells us the customer pays at least 40 days late, so a slowing customer is not mistaken for a punctual one.
        Calendar habitsCustomers who always pay on the last day of the month are forecast that way.
        Sector signalsWhen many customers in one sector slow down, extra delay is added for that sector, scaled by Luxembourg bankruptcy statistics (STATEC).
        The CFO can correct any valueFor example "this customer promised to pay on the 15th". The old value is never overwritten: each correction is recorded with who, when and why.

        Grouped by where they come from. Delays are in days after the invoice due date. Values are those computed on 28 September; in daily use they are recomputed every week.

        2 · Track record: has the forecast been right?

        How well the forecast did same table for every scenario

        For every scenario, the forecast made on 28 September is compared with what really happened in the hidden future. Targets were written down before the engine was built; H1 and H2 were generated only after the engine was frozen and were run once.

        Did it warn in time? The week the forecast first falls below the safety level, against the week cash really fell below it. warned in time = same week or earlier. missed = cash fell below and the forecast did not see it. "none / none" = no shortfall, and none forecast: correct.
        Notice before real shortfall Warning lead time: weeks between the forecast date (28 September) and the week cash really fell below the safety level, when the warning came in time.
        False alarms Weeks the forecast showed below the safety level while real cash stayed above it. Zero is best.
        Error after 1 and 13 weeks Forecast balance minus real balance, in euros and as % of the real balance. + = forecast too optimistic, − = too cautious. When real cash is close to zero, a modest euro gap becomes a large %.
        Real balance inside the likely range Share of the 13 weeks where the real balance fell inside the forecast's range. Ideal is about 80%; lower means the forecast is too sure of itself.

        What works: on unseen data (H1) the shortfall was flagged one week before it happened; in S2 it was flagged in the exact week.

        Known limits: a sudden sector collapse is detected as a warning but its size is underestimated (S3, H2); warnings close to the safety level can stay on for 2–3 weeks; the likely range is narrower than it should be.
